Problem

The Wrong Order

Stage 1 — The Wrong Order

Most people don’t fail bodybuilding because they’re lazy. They fail because they run the year backwards.

They chase muscle before the midsection is honest. They collect programmes like trading cards. They negotiate with themselves every Tuesday night and call it “listening to their body.” Then twelve months later the photos look familiar, the wardrobe still lies, and they swear genetics are the villain.

Here’s the uncomfortable version: the order of operations matters more than your favourite exercise. Get the order wrong and even “hard work” becomes expensive entertainment.

What “the wrong order” looks like

You’ve seen it. You might be living it.

Someone with a soft middle decides the answer is a dirty bulk “so I have something to cut later.” Someone skinny-fat lives at accidental maintenance while hopping from ab challenges to advanced techniques. Someone overweight crashes calories for three weeks, rebound-eats for two, then buys a new programme PDF because the last one “didn’t work.” Someone already tired of starting over adds a supplement stack, a fasting window, and a random advanced tool before they can hit protein four days in a row.

Different costumes. Same mistake: building on a soft base, without a locked plan, without anyone keeping them honest.

The mirror doesn’t care how long you’ve been “into fitness.” It cares what you repeatedly do — and in what sequence.

Scene: the bulk that bought you a bigger soft photo

It’s January. You’re frustrated. A mate says you need to “eat big to get big.” You take that as permission. Training feels heroic because the weights move when you’re full. The scale climbs. Clothes get tighter in the wrong places first. By winter you’re stronger — and the midsection owns every candid photo. You tell yourself you’ll cut “when the time is right.” The time never arrives cleanly, because cutting from a soft surplus is harder than the podcast made it sound, and by then you’re tired of being hungry and tired of being soft.

That’s not a character flaw. That’s the wrong order wearing a surplus hoodie.

Scene: the skinny-fat maintenance fog

You’re not huge. You’re not lean. Arms look okay in a hoodie; shirt off tells a different story. You bounce between “I should bulk” and “I should cut,” so you do neither with intent. You eat by vibe. You train hard sometimes. You change your split when a reel makes you doubt yourself. Six months later the scale hasn’t moved much and neither has the silhouette. You call it a plateau. It’s often just accidental maintenance plus novelty.

Scene: the crash-and-rebound loop

You go hard. Coffee for breakfast. Tiny dinners. Steps through the roof. Week two you look “flatter.” Week three you’re ravenous and social life nukes the deficit. Week five you’ve “failed the diet,” so you buy a new plan to feel in control again. The midsection barely changed. What changed is your trust in yourself.

Scene: tools before boring basics

You’re curious — fair enough. Curiosity without a system becomes a shopping list: powder, pills, gadgets, maybe something more aggressive you saw online. Meanwhile protein is a coin flip and your programme has no logbook. Complexity feels like commitment. It’s often cosplay for the hard parts: food average, progressive sets, sleep, and someone who asks whether you did what you said.

Mistake one: muscle-first when fat still owns the photo

Muscle is the fun story. Fat loss is the adult conversation.

When body fat is high enough to blur every hard edge, “getting bigger” often just makes you a larger version of the same silhouette. You can get stronger. You can even add some tissue. But if the primary visual problem is the midsection, shoulders won’t fix the photo by magic. Spot reduction won’t either. Endless core work won’t.

Fat loss isn’t moral purity. It’s stored energy with a public relations problem — and it moves when average intake sits below average expenditure for long enough that you can still train. That’s not vibes. That’s the boring physics underneath every dramatic transformation collage.

The wrong order pretends you can skip the boring physics because you’re “building a foundation of muscle first.” Sometimes a true hardgainer with very low body fat genuinely needs food and progressive lifting more than a cut — we’ll mark that as a documented exception later, not the default religion. For most people reading this book, especially if you carry meaningful fat or look soft through the middle, stripping fat first is the move that makes the next eleven months readable.

If you’ve ever lost a pile of scale weight and still hated the mirror, you already know: smaller isn’t the same as better. Skinny-fat is what happens when the number drops and the structure never arrives. The wrong order also creates that — crash, vanish, undereat protein, skip hard training, then wonder why you look deflated.

Skinny-fat vs bulk mistakes (say it plainly)

Skinny-fat mistake: assuming low scale weight means you’re ready to dirty bulk. You’re not. Soft middle + thin limbs usually wants honest fat-down or recomp with hard lifting and high protein — not pizza surplus “for aesthetics later.”

Bulk mistake: using “I’m building muscle” as cover for unstructured overeating while the waist climbs faster than your numbers. Surplus can be a tool later. On a soft photo it’s often just delayed honesty.

Crash-cut mistake: chasing the scale so hard you gut training quality and protein, then bounce. You get a temporary number and a long-term identity story that “diets don’t work.”

The physique you want is composition: less of what blurs you, more of what shapes you. Wrong order treats those as separate hobbies instead of a sequence.

Mistake two: programme-hopping dressed up as optimisation

Programme-hopping feels like progress because novelty spikes motivation. Your brain loves new PDFs the way it loves new diets.

Week one: perfect. Week three: “I need more volume.” Week five: a reel says frequency is everything. Week seven: you’re on a different split with different lifts and no logbook continuity. You didn’t plateau. You never stayed still long enough to adapt.

Bodybuilding rewards boring continuity with progressive overload: same movement patterns, logged loads and reps, slightly harder over time. The person who runs one decent programme for twelve weeks will usually beat the person who “researches” twelve programmes for twelve weeks.

The wrong order treats the plan as optional flavour. The right order treats the plan as the job.

What hopping costs in real life

Every restart burns the skill you were building: groove on a squat pattern, familiarity with a progression rule, the quiet confidence of knowing Tuesday’s session before you walk in. Busy people — long shifts, kids, software deadlines — don’t have spare cognitive budget for redesigning the week every Sunday night. Hopping spends that budget on theatre.

You’ll also lose the only honest diagnostic: did this template stop working, or did you stop running it? If you never finish a block, you never find out.

Mistake three: no accountability (negotiating with yourself)

You are a biased committee. On Monday you’re disciplined. On Thursday you’re tired, hungry, and suddenly very interested in “flexible dieting” that somehow never includes the protein target.

Accountability isn’t punishment. It’s an external brain that doesn’t care about your mood. A coach, a training partner, a ruthless mate who asks “did you hit the steps?” — someone who makes the plan real outside your head.

Without that link, most people rewrite the plan mid-week and call it strategy. Missed sessions become “recovery.” Skipped protein becomes “intuitive eating.” Soft photos become “water weight” forever.

If that stings, good. It means you recognise the pattern.

What absence of accountability actually feels like

It feels like freedom for about a fortnight. Then it feels like fog. You vaguely “know” what you should do. You don’t send the check-in. Nobody asks. The missed session doesn’t get logged as a miss — it evaporates. Three months later you can’t explain where the time went, only that you’re “still trying.”

Trying without a witness is where years hide.

Lone-wolf pride is fine until your photos disagree. Independence that quietly deletes targets isn’t virtue; it’s privacy for inconsistency. The fix isn’t shame. It’s a human loop: report, review, adjust one lever, continue.

Mistake four: tools before systems

Supplements, gadgets, and advanced pharmacology can matter in the right context. They are terrible first moves.

If you can’t log training and hit protein for a boring block, adding complexity multiplies mess. The feed flattened enhanced timelines next to natural beginners and sold impatience as education. Curiosity is fine. Chaos is optional.

We’ll cover advanced-tools literacy later as an appendix — what things are, what they aren’t, why unsupervised chaos fails — without turning this book into a protocol shop. For Stage 1, remember: tools amplify the system you already have. If the system is wrong-order chaos, amplification doesn’t save you.

Scene: the stack that didn’t move the waist

You buy the tubs. You feel serious. Training is still inconsistent. Dinner is still guesswork. Three months later your kitchen looks committed and your midsection looks familiar. The lesson isn’t “supplements are fake.” The lesson is order: systems first, tools later, literacy before impulse. Wrong order loves shopping because shopping feels like progress without demanding Tuesday’s protein.

The quiet fifth mistake: measuring the wrong thing

Wrong order often pairs with wrong scoreboard. Scale-only thinking celebrates crash cuts. Mirror-only thinking without standards celebrates flattering light. Programme-count thinking celebrates PDFs owned instead of blocks finished.

Useful scoreboard for a year that matters: same-condition photos, waist, strength logs, adherence. Everything else is optional colour. If your scoreboard rewards novelty and punishment, you’ll keep buying novelty and punishment.

Why twelve months still matters

Short challenges create short people. Twelve months is long enough for fat loss phases, skill in the gym, real strength, and photos that don’t lie — if the sequence is right.

It’s also long enough to waste if you spend it hopping.

This book assumes you have a real life: work, maybe kids, imperfect sleep, social meals, stress that doesn’t care about your hypertrophy goals. The wrong order usually comes from trying to out-clever that life with novelty. The right order installs a sequence that survives ordinary weeks.

Twelve months isn’t magic because of the number. It’s useful because it forces you past the honeymoon. Motivation dies; systems don’t have to. A year of wrong order teaches you the bitter story. A year of right order teaches you what your body does when you stop interrupting it.

Think of the year as four seasons of behaviour, not twelve separate New Year’s resolutions. Wrong order restarts every season. Right order compounds.

The cost of waiting another year

Another year of wrong order doesn’t just delay the physique. It teaches your identity the wrong lesson: “nothing works.” That story is usually false. What failed was sequence, adherence, and honesty — not your bones.

Cost of waiting:

  • More photos you’ll hide.
  • More programmes you’ll abandon.
  • More money on things that don’t move the midsection.
  • More comparison to people who simply stayed on one plan longer than you stayed curious.
  • Another birthday with the same soft middle story and a harder restart because trust in yourself took another hit.
  • More “I’ll start when life calms down” — life rarely calendars itself around your cut.
  • Another winter of hoodies as strategy.
  • Another summer of “next summer” promises.
  • Another round of explaining to yourself why this wasn’t the right time — while the midsection quietly becomes the default you stop noticing until a photo ambushes you.

Waiting feels responsible when you frame it as research. Sometimes research is stalling with better vocabulary. You already know enough to take baseline photos, lock calories and protein, write a twelve-week template, and name a human who will ask. The rest of this book makes that sequence sharper. It does not replace the decision to begin.

There’s also a social cost people skip: you start believing you’re “the unfit mate,” the one who jokes first so nobody else has to. Humour is fine. Using it as a permanent escape hatch from sequence is not. Twelve months of right order won’t fix your entire life, but it will retire a lot of that defensive comedy because the photos stop agreeing with the joke.

You don’t need athlete genetics. You need the right order, a plan you’ll still run in week eight, and a human who notices when you quietly quit.

One more honest inventory

Before Stage 2 names the method, sit with four questions — not as shame, as diagnostics:

  1. Is fat still owning the photo while you chase “getting bigger”?
  2. Have you finished a twelve-week programme without rewriting it into a different religion?
  3. Does anyone outside your head know your targets and ask about them?
  4. Are you adding tools faster than you’re logging basics?

If you answered yes / no / no / yes, congratulations: you have met the wrong order. Most of us have. The point of this stage isn’t to roast you. It’s to stop you spending another expensive year rehearsing the same play with new costumes.

Wrong order is common because it’s culturally rewarded: bulk culture, novelty culture, lone-wolf culture, tool culture. Right order feels slower in week one and faster across the year. This book bets on the year.

If you recognise yourself in more than one scene, that’s normal. Wrong order rarely arrives as a single cartoon mistake. It’s usually a stack: soft bulk and hopping and no check-in and a tub you bought instead of a logbook. Untangle the stack by fixing sequence first. Everything else gets easier when the year isn’t running backwards.

One last scene, because it belongs here: the competent person who runs systems at work and improvises their body. Long shifts. Kids. A calendar that never asks permission. They don’t lack grit — they lack a locked order that survives Thursday. Wrong order thrives in busy lives precisely because novelty and negotiation feel like control when time is scarce. Right order is the opposite kind of control: fewer decisions, clearer sequence, a human who asks.

Solution

The STRIPLINE Method

Stage 2 — The STRIPLINE Method

In twelve months you’ll change how you look by stripping fat first, locking one plan, and building under accountability — without bulk-before-you’re-lean chaos or another abandoned programme PDF.

That sentence is the whole product. Everything else is implementation — weeks, reviews, and the refusal to hop when boredom asks politely.

The STRIPLINE Method has four moves: STRIP → LINE → LIFT → LINK. They aren’t motivational posters. They’re the order of operations for The 12-Month Body — four moves, one spine, no competing brand.

Old gym roadmaps still talk about Foundation, Build, Refine, Peak. Those can still describe months inside the year. They are not distinctive enough as a method, and they often smuggle in “pick your calorie religion” before they’ve told you to get the midsection honest. STRIPLINE fixes the spine: fat-loss-first by default, one locked plan, muscle emphasis when the base deserves it, accountability so you don’t negotiate the plan into dust.

STRIP — fat down before you obsess over getting bigger

STRIP means your first job is getting body fat moving in the right direction until the photo stops lying.

Not “get stage lean in six weeks.” Not punishment cardio until you hate training. A sustainable deficit (or a recomp that still prioritises midsection progress) while you lift hard and eat enough protein that you don’t evaporate.

What STRIP looks like in practice

  • Calories set for fat loss (or honest recomp) — weekly average matters more than one perfect day.
  • Protein high enough to protect muscle (~1.6–2.2 g per kg as a practical band; use goal weight if body weight is very high).
  • Progressive resistance training kept non-negotiable — you’re not “just dieting.”
  • Steps / daily movement as a quiet fat-loss helper.
  • Photos + waist + strength as the scoreboard; scale as supporting actor.

STRIP is where most people should spend early months even if their ego wants a bulk. Soft middles don’t become athletic by eating in a surplus and hoping shoulders outrun the belly.

A week in the life of STRIP (ordinary adult edition)

Monday: train upper or full body as written. Hit protein. Steps on the watch. Sleep defended even if the day ran long.

Tuesday: maybe legs or a second session. Hunger shows up mid-afternoon — you don’t invent a new diet; you eat the plan’s meal or a default high-protein option you already decided exists.

Wednesday: rest or active recovery. Steps still count. No “I earned a free-for-all because I trained hard Monday.”

Thursday: train. Log loads. Notice one lift moved a rep — that’s the quiet win STRIP protects.

Friday: social meal in the diary. You don’t cancel the week; you bank calories earlier or accept a weekly average that still trends down.

Weekend: kids, overtime, life. Bad-week minimum ready: protein floor, two short sessions if needed, step floor, no programme rewrite.

That’s STRIP. Not aesthetic suffering. Direction with a life attached.

STRIP examples by starting point

Higher body fat: obvious. Modest deficit, high protein, lift like muscle matters, steps up. Expect clothes and waist to talk before the mirror becomes flattering under bad lighting.

Skinny-fat: often a lighter deficit or recomp — still midsection-first. Don’t dirty bulk because you feel “small.” Small and soft is not the same problem as lean and underfed.

Restarter: you’ve lifted before. STRIP still wins if the photo is soft. Experience doesn’t exempt you from physics; it just means LINE can reuse skills faster once you stop hopping.

The lean-hardgainer exception (documented — not the default)

If you are already lean, light-framed, and genuinely struggle to eat enough — photos show little fat to strip — your STRIP phase may be short or mostly “stop cutting yourself into nothing.” You still LINE (lock a plan). Your LIFT may arrive sooner with a small surplus. Write the exception down. Don’t use “I’m a hardgainer” as cosplay while carrying a soft midsection.

How to document the exception properly

  1. Same-condition photos: front, side, back. Good light optional; consistency mandatory.
  2. Waist measure. If the midsection is clearly soft, you do not get the exception.
  3. One-sentence rationale: “Already lean; underfed history; priority is progressive surplus under a locked plan.”
  4. Review date in 4–6 weeks. If waist climbs faster than strength and limb fullness, you lied to yourself — return to STRIP without drama.

True hardgainers often undereat relative to training, fear “getting fat,” and live in a cut mentality that keeps them small forever. The exception is permission to feed the work — not permission to eat chaos. LINE still locks calories upward with intent. LINK still asks whether you hit them.

If you’re unsure, default to STRIP/recomp until the photo votes. Ego prefers the bulk story. Honesty prefers evidence.

LINE — lock one plan and stop hopping

LINE means you draw the line: this is the programme, these are the food targets, these are the sleep/step floors, these are the review dates. Then you run it.

A programme is not a religion. It’s a way to organise hard sets across the week so muscles get trained often enough, recovered enough, and progressed enough around your actual calendar. One boring template beaten for twelve weeks beats three clever templates abandoned at week three.

What gets locked

  1. Training template — days, lifts, progression rule (e.g. double progression: add reps in a range, then add load).
  2. Nutrition targets — calories + protein at minimum; carbs/fats fill the rest in a way you can digest, train on, and repeat.
  3. Recovery floors — sleep minimum you’ll defend; deload rule.
  4. Movement floor — steps especially in STRIP.
  5. Review ritual — every 2–4 weeks: photos, waist, strength, adherence. Change one lever if stalled.

LINE is also where you stop collecting rules. Meal timing, fancy supplements, advanced intensity techniques — seasoning. Calories, protein, progressive overload, sleep — the meal.

If a diet style tanks your adherence every time you try it, stop collecting rematches with the same fad. Adherence is the cost centre that decides whether any approach works.

A week in the life of LINE

Sunday night (ten minutes): glance at the written week. No redesign. If travel exists, swap days — don’t invent a new philosophy.

Training days: same lifts in the same order unless pain forces a substitution you already listed as an alternate. Logbook open before the first warm-up set.

Food: defaults ready — breakfast protein, work lunch that hits the number, dinner that doesn’t require chef theatre. Tracking if you need it; averages if daily weighing makes you mental.

Review Friday or every second Sunday: adherence score honest. Missed two sessions? That’s data. Not a personality verdict. One lever only if stalled: calories, steps, or a progression tweak — not all three plus a new split.

LINE examples

Shift worker: lock a 3-day full-body rotating on available days, not “Mon/Wed/Fri” fantasy. Protein targets stay; timing flexes.

Parent evenings: shorter sessions with the big lifts first. Optional accessories only if time remains. LINE protects the core, not the Instagram accessory list.

Office + gym after work: commute and hunger are the enemies. Pre-packed protein and a fixed start time beat “I’ll go if I feel like it.”

LIFT — build muscle on a cleaner base

LIFT is progressive overload with intent: more load, more reps, better control, or more quality hard sets over time — not random sweating.

You train during STRIP too. LIFT as a phase emphasis means: once the midsection trajectory is honest (or your documented exception applies), you bias more of the year toward muscle gain — food up toward maintenance or a small surplus, volume progression where recovery allows, weak-point work without novelty addiction.

What LIFT is not

  • Ego maxing every session because you feel motivated.
  • Copying advanced volume from someone who recovers like a professional athlete with a nap schedule.
  • Abandoning protein and sleep because “I’m bulking now.”

Muscle grows when you give it a reason, the raw materials, and enough recovery to finish the job. Training is the request. Recovery is the construction. Food decides whether the request is easy, hard, or ignored.

A week in the life of LIFT emphasis

Calories sit near maintenance or a small surplus you chose on purpose. Hunger is less dramatic; the job is hitting protein and not turning every weekend into an untracked surplus avalanche.

Sessions push progression: add a rep, add a kilo, improve the pause, clean up the last set. Weak points get one or two honest movements — not a new programme identity.

Sleep and deloads matter more because volume may creep. LINK asks whether you’re actually recovering or just eating more and calling it a bulk.

LIFT examples

After a successful STRIP: waist down, strength mostly held, photos cleaner — raise food, keep the same template, chase overload for 8–12 weeks.

Hardgainer exception: small surplus from week one of the locked plan; patience measured in months of logged progression, not fortnightly scale panic.

Still soft at month six: you don’t “LIFT harder” by force. You keep stripping or recomping. LIFT emphasis is earned by criteria, not calendar boredom.

What LINK looks like weekly

  • You send: sessions done, protein hit?, steps, one sentence on hunger/energy.
  • They ask: did you follow the written plan — not the rewritten Thursday version?
  • You review photos on schedule, not when the lighting is flattering.

LINK also covers Year 2 decisions later: stay in a growth block, another STRIP polish, advanced-tools literacy with supervision — not 1 a.m. impulse after a reel.

A week in the life of LINK

Check-in is boring on purpose. “3/3 sessions. Protein 6/7. Steps average 8k. Sleep rough Wednesday.” Your person replies with one question that hurts a little: “What happens Thursday when you’re tired?” You answer with the MED plan, not a speech.

Monthly photo drop isn’t a performance. Same bathroom, same light if you can, same poses. Comparison to month one beats comparison to strangers.

Without LINK, LINE slowly becomes a suggestion. With LINK, missed weeks get recovered instead of mythologised.

How STRIPLINE maps onto twelve months

Think in phases, not vibes.

Months STRIPLINE bias What “good” looks like
1–3 STRIP + LINE Fat moving or midsection improving; plan locked; lifts logged; habits installed
4–6 STRIP continues or transition Still honest about fat; strength mostly protected; adherence high
7–9 LIFT emphasis when criteria met If midsection allows, push muscle; if not, keep stripping without drama
10–12 LIFT + LINK Photos vs month 1; strength PRs; Year 2 sketch; accountability still on

Inside those months you can still use words like foundation (skill), build (stress), refine (aim), peak (show the work). They are subtitles under STRIPLINE — not a competing brand.

Month mapping examples (stories, not prescriptions)

Example A — soft start: Months 1–6 mostly STRIP + LINE. Waist drops. Strength holds. Months 7–10 LIFT emphasis. Months 11–12 polish, photos, Year 2 plan with LINK still weekly.

Example B — skinny-fat: Months 1–4 recomp/STRIP bias with aggressive protein and lifting. Middle tightens, limbs thicken slightly. Months 5–12 gradual LIFT if criteria met — or continued recomp if the photo still softens under surplus.

Example C — lean hardgainer exception: Month 1 short STRIP-as-“stop undereating,” LINE hard. Months 2–12 LIFT with patient surplus, same template, LINK watching waist so surplus doesn’t become denial.

Example D — restarter with history: Month 1 audit: delete novelty, lock one template, decide STRIP vs LIFT from photos. Then follow the table without reinventing your identity every block.

Your calendar will differ. The spine shouldn’t.

Starting-point modifiers (not alternate religions)

People start different. The method doesn’t.

  • Higher body fat: STRIP is obviously primary. Sustainable deficit, high protein, lift like muscle matters.
  • Skinny-fat: STRIP/recomp until the middle tightens and limbs thicken — don’t dirty bulk “for aesthetics later.”
  • Already lean hardgainer: documented exception; LINE hard; LIFT sooner with patient surplus.
  • Restarter with gym history: LINE means audit and simplify before novelty; then apply STRIP or LIFT based on photos, not ego.

Secondary curiosity about advanced tools never hijacks months 1–3. Fundamentals first. Literacy later.

How the year should feel (not just look)

Months 1–3 often feel annoying: hunger management, learning the template, fewer dopamine hits from novelty. That’s installation, not failure.

Months 4–6 feel quieter if you’re doing it right — less drama, more logged work. People quit here because quiet doesn’t feel like content. Stay.

Months 7–9 either open into LIFT emphasis (food up, chase tissue) or continue honest STRIP without calling yourself broken. Criteria decide, not ego.

Months 10–12 should feel like evidence: photos vs month one, strength story, a Year 2 sketch that isn’t panic. LINK is still on. The year ends with a system, not a cliff.

What you stop doing when STRIPLINE is real

  • Bulking into a soft photo because a podcast said so.
  • Changing programmes when motivation is bored.
  • Using the scale as a courtroom.
  • Quitting protein when appetite is weird.
  • Running unsupervised chaos because someone online “looks insane.”
  • Treating accountability as optional personality.
  • Collecting diet identities instead of weekly averages.
  • Confusing research nights with training nights.
  • Waiting for perfect sleep before you earn average sleep.
  • Using “hardgainer” as a costume for a soft middle.

Common objections (answered briefly)

“Won’t I lose muscle if I strip first?”
Not if protein is high, lifting stays progressive, and the deficit is adult-sized. Panic cuts lose tissue. STRIP isn’t a panic cut.

“I only have three days.”
LINE can be three days. Frequency fantasies don’t beat finished weeks.

“I travel constantly.”
Defaults travel better than perfect kitchens. Protein targets and a shortened session list beat “I’ll restart when I’m home.”

“Accountability feels soft.”
It’s structural. Planes have checklists. Builds have inspections. Your physique can have a check-in without becoming a personality cult.

“What if I stall?”
Review ritual. One lever. Photos and waist before panic. Stalls are information when LINE is intact; they’re identity crises when you’ve hopped too much to know what you were testing.

“Can I do STRIPLINE in a commercial gym with limited kit?”
Yes. LINE picks patterns you can load: squat/hinge/push/pull/carry variants that exist on your floor. Fancy machines are optional. Progressive hard sets are not.

Micro-example: locking LINE in one sitting

Open a note. Write:

  • Days: e.g. Mon / Wed / Fri
  • Session skeleton: squat pattern, hinge, horizontal push, horizontal pull, vertical push or pull, one arm isolation, one carry or core
  • Progression: 3×6–10; add weight when all sets hit 10 clean
  • Calories / protein: from Food Bro PDF or equivalent — numbers on the page

Hardgainer exception — worked detail

Let’s labour this because misuse is common.

You might be a candidate if: visible fat is low in honest photos; waist is already tight; training history shows food fear or chronic under-eating; strength rises when calories rise; “cutting” mostly makes you smaller and colder without revealing much muscle.

You are probably not a candidate if: side photos show a soft middle; belts tell on you; you rebound between “I’m skinny” feelings and hoodie softness; you want a bulk mainly because surplus is more fun than adherence.

What the exception changes: STRIP becomes brief — stop the accidental cut, install LINE, move toward a small surplus sooner.

What the exception does not change: you still lock a programme; you still log progressive overload; you still review waist so surplus doesn’t become denial; you still LINK so “I ate big” isn’t an unverified vibe.

Patient surplus literacy: small and steady beats dirty chaos. If waist races ahead of strength and limb fullness, you didn’t discover hardgainer destiny — you discovered unstructured overeating. Pull back toward maintenance, keep lifting, reassess. The exception is a lane, not a personality.

Month sketch for a documented exception: Month 1 — photos, LINE lock, food up from undereating toward a planned surplus, LINK weekly. Plateau one-lever tree

Definition of a real plateau: adherence is genuinely high for ~2–4 weeks and photos/waist/strength are stuck or moving the wrong way.

If adherence is low, it is not a plateau. Fix compliance.

Tree (change one lever, then re-review in 2–4 weeks)

  1. Steps / NEAT
    Raise daily step floor by ~1–2k (STRIP) or restore a collapsed floor (any phase). Silent NEAT drops sabotage deficits.

  2. Calories (small)
    STRIP stalled fat loss → trim ~100–200 kcal/day or tighten weekend logging accuracy first if leaks are obvious.
    Strength collapsing / misery → raise ~100–200 kcal.
    LIFT waist sprinting → trim surplus; do not “train more” as the first lie.

  3. Sleep / stress load
    Defend sleep floor; cut junk volume; use MED sessions for 1 week if life is on fire.

  4. Training honesty
    Are work sets actually near failure with decent form? Is progression logged? Add one hard set to a lagging lift or clean up RIR — not both plus a new split.

  5. Only then consider structure
    Swap an exercise for a pain-free alternate; deload; or change days to match the calendar. Programme-hop as first response is how you stay stuck with better branding.

Hard rule: one lever. Write it down. Give it time. Then review.

Worked plateau cases

Case A — fake plateau: “Nothing’s happening.” Digging shows 3/7 protein days and two skipped sessions. Action: MED week to reset habits; no calorie change; no new split.

Case B — STRIP stall with good adherence: waist flat 3 weeks, photos unchanged, lifts holding, steps drifted from 9k → 5k. Action: restore step floor first. Re-review in 3 weeks.

Case C — STRIP stall, steps good: logging verified, steps solid, waist flat, mild strength rise. Action: trim ~150 kcal/day. Hold programme.

Case D — LIFT waist creep: surplus on paper looks small, sleeves improving, waist +2 cm in a month, weekend logging sparse. Action: fix weekend logging accuracy for 2 weeks before cutting food; if still climbing, trim surplus.

Case E — strength crash on cut: photos improving but every lift down and sleep poor. Action: raise calories, sleep floor, optional 1-week volume MED — not “add more intensity techniques.”

Stage 5B — Calories, Macros & Tracking

Food is not a personality. It’s the metering system for fat loss and muscle growth.

If you lift hard and eat like the goal is optional, your body shrugs. If you obsess over meal timing while your weekly calories are chaos, your body also shrugs — just with more spreadsheets.

This stage is the nutrition system for The 12-Month Body: what calories do in STRIP and LIFT, how to set protein/carbs/fats without cult behaviour, how to track like an adult with a real job, and how to eat when the day is twelve hours long and dinner is whatever is left in the fridge.

Keep Stage 2’s sequence in your head. STRIPLINE decides the phase. This chapter decides the numbers and the logistics.

What calories are — and how they map to STRIP vs LIFT

A calorie is a unit of energy. Your body takes energy in (food and drink) and spends energy out (basal metabolism, digestion, training, steps, fidgeting, stress, sleep debt, the lot). Over weeks, the relationship between average intake and average expenditure decides whether body mass trends down, holds, or climbs.

That is not a vibe. It is not “eating clean.” Clean food can still overfeed you. Dirty food can still sit inside a deficit. Food quality matters for health, hunger, training performance, and whether you can stick the plan — but the fat-on-the-midsection lever is still average energy balance.

Maintenance

Maintenance is the average daily intake that keeps your trend weight roughly stable over a few weeks when training and steps are similar.

It is not one sacred number forever. It moves with:

  • body size (bigger bodies usually burn more)
  • muscle mass and training load
  • daily steps / NEAT (non-exercise activity — pottering, pacing, standing, fidgeting)
  • sleep and stress
  • how aggressive the previous phase was

Treat maintenance as a working estimate, then let the scale trend and photos referee it.

A useful mental model: maintenance is a range, not a laser line. Two weeks at ±100 kcal around the true average often look like “holding.” That is why tiny daily obsessing is wasted effort and weekly averages are the grown-up tool.

Why the scale lies short-term (and why you still use it)

Use the scale. Do not worship it.

Short-term weight shifts come from:

  • Glycogen + water — carbs and hard training pull water into muscle; low-carb days dump some of it
  • Sodium and restaurant meals — hold extra water without adding fat overnight
  • Gut content — high-fibre days and big meals sit on the scale
  • Inflammation / soreness — heavy lower-body sessions can bump morning weight
  • Hormonal cycle (where relevant) — water retention patterns across the month
  • Stress and sleep — cortisol/sleep debt can change water handling and hunger

Fat loss still shows up as a downward trend across weeks when the deficit is real. Fat gain still shows up as an upward trend when the surplus is real. Your job is to stop treating a single weigh-in like a court verdict.

Practical weigh-in protocol:

  1. Same conditions most mornings (after bathroom, before food/drink, similar clothing).
  2. Record the number without emotional commentary.
  3. Average the week.
  4. Compare week-to-week averages alongside photos and waist.

Deficit (STRIP default)

A deficit means average intake sits below what you need to hold weight. Fat loss requires this over time. STRIP is fat-loss-first for anyone carrying meaningful fat or skinny-fat softness — so most readers start here.

Practical starting point for many busy adults:

Phase intent Starting calorie adjustment
STRIP fat loss ~300–500 kcal/day below estimated maintenance
Larger bodies / faster early drop (if hunger and training hold) sometimes nearer −500 to −700, then moderate
Too aggressive (strength collapsing, misery, adherence dying) raise calories; heroics are not a strategy

Expect noisy weeks. Water, glycogen, salt, gut content, training soreness, and hormones move the scale more than fat does day to day. Judge the weekly average, photos, waist, and strength — not Tuesday morning panic.

STRIP still wants high protein and progressive lifting. The goal is not “smaller soft person.” The goal is less fat while you keep (and ideally improve) the muscle signal.

How aggressive should the deficit be? Aggressive enough that photos change over a month. Gentle enough that you can still train, sleep, work, and not hate your life. If your only personality trait on a cut becomes irritability and skipped sessions, you did not find discipline — you found a deficit that is too deep for your current recovery budget.

STRIP calorie reality checks:

  • Early weeks often drop faster (water/glycogen). Do not assume that rate continues.
  • If waist and photos improve while scale weight stalls briefly, you may be recomposing or holding water — stay the course before slashing again.
  • If strength is free-falling and workouts are junk for two weeks, raise food or cut volume before you “add more cardio punishment.”

Surplus (LIFT emphasis — when earned)

A surplus means average intake sits above maintenance so gaining tissue is easier. In STRIPLINE, this is not month-one cosplay for someone who still has a midsection story to finish. LIFT calories are earned when photos and adherence say the middle is ready enough — or when a true hardgainer exception was documented honestly.

Practical starting surplus for many:

Phase intent Starting calorie adjustment
LIFT growth ~200–400 kcal/day above maintenance
Impatient dirty bulk usually buys extra fat once a modest surplus already covers growth

Patience beats chaos. Soft ≠ failure if strength is climbing and you still have months on the clock. Aborting a growth phase at week eight because the mirror got slightly softer is how people stay forever small-soft and confused.

In a surplus, many people unconsciously move more (NEAT rises — pacing, fidgeting, general pottering). That means a chunk of “extra” calories does not always become fat one-for-one. Still: do not use NEAT as an excuse to eat like a hardgainer meme and wonder why nothing happens. Track steps so the silent changes are visible.

LIFT calorie reality checks:

  • Aim for slow scale drift upward over weeks, not a revenge bulk.
  • Keep protein high so the surplus has somewhere useful to go.
  • If waist is racing ahead of strength for several weeks, trim 100–200 kcal.
  • Appetite can be a skill — scheduled feeds and liquid calories help when chewing becomes the bottleneck.

Near-maintenance / recomp

Some stretches sit near maintenance: skill-building weeks, transitions between STRIP and LIFT, or skinny-fat phases where protein, lifting, and steps do the quiet work while scale weight barely moves. Photos and waist still matter more than the decimal on the scale.

Recomp is not magic and it is not an excuse to avoid deciding. It is a useful holding pattern when:

  • you are new enough that muscle and fat can move usefully near maintenance
  • you are bridging phases without chaos
  • life stress says “do not open a deep deficit or surplus right now”

If you have a clear midsection problem and keep “recomping” for nine months with soft photos, you are usually just avoiding STRIP.

Quick map

Energy setting STRIPLINE job What “winning” looks like
Deficit STRIP Waist/photos improve; strength holds or rises slowly; adherence real
Maintenance ± nudge Hold / recomp / transition Composition improves without big scale drama
Surplus LIFT (earned) Strength and size climb; fat gain stays controlled

If you only remember one sentence: calories set the phase; protein and training protect the look.

Why weekly averages beat daily drama

Fat loss and fat gain are slow relative to water shifts. You can “gain” a kilo overnight from a salty takeaway and glycogen refill without gaining a kilo of fat. You can “lose” fast in week one of a cut mostly from glycogen and gut content.

So:

  • Weigh most mornings (or several times per week) under similar conditions if the scale doesn’t wreck your head.
  • Average the week.
  • Pair that average with waist and photos every 2–4 weeks.
  • Ask: is the midsection story moving the way STRIP or LIFT intends?

If the scale is a mental landmine for you, lean harder on waist + photos + how work trousers fit, and use weight less often. The goal is decision-quality data, not a daily emotional flogging.

NEAT — the silent calorie thief (and gift)

When you eat in a surplus, many people unconsciously move more: pacing, fidgeting, taking the stairs, pottering. When you diet hard, some people become quiet energy-savers without noticing — fewer steps, more sitting, less spontaneous movement.

That is why step targets matter in STRIP. They keep expenditure from silently collapsing while you cut food. In LIFT, endless cardio that erases a carefully built surplus is equally silly. Match movement to the phase instead of collecting random punishment sessions.

Protein, carbs, and fats — what they do, and practical ranges

Macros are the three big energy nutrients: protein, carbohydrate, and fat. Alcohol is the awkward fourth that still counts.

You do not need a PhD. You need a hierarchy you will not invert.

The hierarchy (memorise this)

  1. Average calories vs your needs — deficit to lose fat (STRIP default), near-maintenance to recomp/hold, surplus when LIFT is earned.
  2. Protein — high enough to support muscle in whatever phase you’re in.
  3. Carbs and fats — fill the rest in a way you can digest, train on, and repeat.
  4. Micronutrients, fibre, hydration — health and adherence glue.
  5. Meal timing / food “rules” — useful seasoning, rarely the main meal.

Most beginners invert this list. They buy a fat burner before they know if they’re eating 1,800 or 3,800 by accident.

Protein

Protein supplies amino acids for repair and growth. In a deficit it helps you hold muscle and stay fuller. In a surplus it still matters — extra carbs do not replace it.

Practical daily band for resistance trainees:

~1.6–2.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day
(about 0.7–1 g per lb as a simple high heuristic)

Notes that keep this sane:

  • If body weight is very high, use goal weight or an adjusted body weight so the target is achievable.
  • In STRIP, lean toward the higher end of the band when hunger and retention need help.
  • In LIFT, still hit a solid total; surplus carbs are fuel, not a protein substitute.
  • Distribute across 3–5 feedings if you can. Perfect timing is optional; daily total is not.

Protein powder is convenience food, not magic. Matched grams from yoghurt, meat, eggs, or powder do the same job for hitting the number.

Fats

Dietary fat supports hormone production, fat-soluble vitamins, cell membranes, and — bluntly — whether food tastes like something you’ll eat for twelve months.

Loose practical band: ~0.6–1 g of fat per kg of body weight as a starting floor/ceiling guide for many people. Do not crush fats to zero to “make room for carbs.” Do not flood fats so high that protein and training carbs get squeezed out of a modest calorie budget.

Choose mostly unsaturated sources you tolerate (olive oil, avocado, nuts, fatty fish) and keep room for foods you actually enjoy. Exact split can flex with preference.

Carbohydrates

Carbs fill glycogen, support hard training, and are often the easiest lever for energy once protein and fats are set. Fruit, potatoes, rice, oats, bread, pasta — none of these are morally superior or evil. They are tools.

Practical rule after protein and fats are set:

Calories remaining ÷ 4 ≈ carbohydrate grams for the day.

Bias more carbs around hard training sessions if performance feels better that way. On rest days you can keep carbs similar for simplicity, or nudge them down slightly if it helps hunger control in STRIP — consistency beats clever micro-timing.

Putting macros into one picture

Once calories and protein are locked, fats and carbs are mostly preference + performance:

Situation Bias that often helps
Hard lower-body / high-volume training days More carbs around the session
Very low appetite in LIFT Liquid carbs/milk, fruit, rice; keep protein non-negotiable
STRIP hunger loud at night Save a satisfying protein + carb dinner; don’t “save” all food for 9 p.m. chaos
Digestion issues with high fat Lower fat toward ~0.6 g/kg; raise carbs
Digests fats well, hates huge rice portions Raise fats toward ~1 g/kg; carbs fill less

There is no trophy for the most complicated split. The trophy is still hitting the weekly average twelve months from now.

Micronutrients without becoming a supplement catalogue

Hit most of this from food:

  • colourful plants across the week
  • some dairy or fortified alternative if you tolerate it
  • fish a couple of times a week when possible (or discuss omega-3 options later in the tools section of the book)
  • iodised salt / normal varied meals rather than fear of every mineral

Supplements can fill gaps. They do not excuse a diet of three foods and hope. Creatine and basics belong in the tools conversation later; here, food structure comes first.

Fibre, veggies, hydration

These are not optional “health fluff.” Fibre and high-volume vegetables make STRIP hunger manageable. Water makes everything less miserable. If your diet is only protein shakes and dry chicken with no plants, expect constipation, cravings, and a mood that belongs in a parking-fine queue.

A practical hydration default for many adults is drinking enough that urine is pale most of the day — more in heat, on site, or in hard training — without turning yourself into a water-chugging mascot. Pair sodium and fluids like a normal eater, not a chemistry experiment, unless a coach or clinician has you on a specific protocol [PLACEHOLDER: coach-specific detail].

Alcohol

Alcohol is energy. Rough guide many people use: ~7 kcal per gram, before mixers. It also lowers inhibition, wrecks sleep for some people, and turns “I’ll estimate” into “I forgot three drinks.” Budget it. Log it. Don’t build your identity around purity theatre or nightly obliteration.

If you drink, prefer a planned number inside the weekly calorie average over a surprise blackout of logging. Beer and sugary mixers add up fast; spirits with zero/low-cal mixers are easier to budget — still not free.

How to set your numbers — step by step

No mysticism. Estimate → choose phase → run → review.

Step 1 — Get a maintenance estimate

Pick one starter method (both are fine):

Method A — calculator + humility
Enter your stats into a reputable TDEE/calorie estimator (or the metering tool in the next section). Treat the output as a guess, not a prophecy.

Method B — reverse engineer from real life
Track intake honestly for 10–14 days while eating “normally.” Average daily calories. Watch weekly average weight. If weight is roughly flat, that intake is near maintenance. If weight is rising or falling, adjust the estimate accordingly.

Many people do A to start, then correct with B within two to four weeks.

Step 2 — Choose the phase (STRIPLINE decides)

Your situation Default calorie setting
Meaningful fat to lose / skinny-fat soft middle STRIP deficit
Midsection ready enough; adherence high; ready to grow LIFT surplus (earned)
Transition, skill block, or honest recomp stretch Near maintenance
True hardgainer with low fat and poor appetite Document the exception; often a patient surplus — not “dirty bulk because impatient”

Write the daily calorie target in your logbook. Round to something you can hit (±50–100 kcal is normal noise).

Step 3 — Set protein first

  1. Choose body weight or adjusted/goal weight in kg.
  2. Multiply by 1.6–2.2.
  3. Write the gram target.
  4. Build meals that make that number boringly inevitable.

Example band for an 85 kg trainee: roughly 136–187 g protein/day. Pick a number in that band you can hit on workdays.

Step 4 — Set fats, then fill carbs

  1. Set fats around 0.6–1 g/kg (loose).
  2. Convert protein and fat grams to calories (protein × 4, fat × 9).
  3. Subtract from daily calorie target.
  4. Remaining calories ÷ 4 = carbohydrate grams.

If the carb number looks tiny and you train hard, nudge fats down within the band rather than nuking protein.

Step 5 — Run 2–4 weeks before rewriting your life

Change one big lever at a time. Hit the targets. Lift. Sleep enough to function. Walk.

How to track — step by step

Tracking is literacy, not a personality transplant. You are learning what portions actually are. Most people are shocked in week one. That shock is useful.

If tracking food is psychologically rough because of a disordered eating history, get professional support and use a gentler method (portion frameworks, plate method, coach-guided structure). This book is not a substitute for that care.

1) Get a kitchen scale

Digital, grams, cheap is fine. Put it on the bench. Leave it there. A scale you have to dig out of a drawer will not get used on a twelve-hour day.

2) Weigh food the useful way

  • Weigh raw meats consistently or cooked consistently — pick a method and stick to it so the database matches.
  • Weigh calorie-dense foods that are easy to underestimate: oils, peanut butter, cheese, nuts, dressings, butter.
  • Vegetables can be looser once you know your pattern; oils cannot.
  • “A drizzle” is often two tablespoons. Two tablespoons is not a drizzle. The scale ends the debate.

Raw vs cooked tip: 100 g raw chicken is not 100 g cooked chicken. If your app entry says “chicken breast, raw,” weigh it raw. If it says cooked, weigh cooked. Mixing those up is a classic silent error that makes people think maths is broken when their method is.

3) Read labels without getting conned

  • Check serving size before the pretty front of pack.
  • Note whether numbers are per 100 g or per serving.
  • Liquids and “health” bars still count.
  • When cooking mixed dishes, weigh ingredients in, then portion the finished meal by weight if you need accuracy.

Example: a “healthy” granola can be denser than cake once you measure the bowl you actually pour. Front-of-pack marketing is not a macro entry. The nutrition panel is.

4) Pick one logging app and stop shopping for apps

Any decent food logger works if you use it. Search the database carefully (brand + exact product). Save frequent meals as favourites or recipes so busy days take thirty seconds, not thirty minutes.

Build a personal library in week one:

  • your default breakfast
  • two work lunches
  • two dinners
  • your usual shake
  • your usual coffee milk amount

After that, most days are tap-tap-done. Tracking should become boring. Boring is the point.

5) Log as you go — especially the “harmless” bits

Coffee milk. Sauce. Weekend nibbles. The bite of whatever your kids left. Unlogged food is how people swear they eat 1,900 while maintaining at 2,600.

If you hate logging at dinner time, log earlier feeds during the day and only finish the last meal at night. The open loop of “I’ll add it later” is where entire evenings disappear.

6) Use weekly calorie averages — not daily panic

Daily intake will swing. Training days, rest days, social meals, travel. That is normal.

Do this instead:

  1. Log daily.
  2. At week’s end, average the seven days.
  3. Compare average intake to your target.
  4. Compare weekly average weight (or 7-day trend) plus photos/waist/strength.

One high day does not ruin a cut. One low day does not make you “disciplined.” Trends do.

A clean way to think about it: if your target is 2,300 kcal, a week that lands at ~16,100 total (~2,300/day average) is on target even if Wednesday was 2,800 and Thursday was 1,900. Chase the week, not a perfect straight line.

7) Rest days

You still need protein. Calories can match training days for simplicity, or sit slightly lower if it helps STRIP hunger — [PLACEHOLDER: coach-specific detail] on exact rest-day carb protocol if you use one. What matters is that rest days are logged, not treated as free space outside the dataset.

Rest days are also where “I earned a blowout because I trained hard yesterday” turns into quiet maintenance. Keep the structure. Keep protein. Keep walking if STRIP is the job.

8) Eating out — estimate like an adult

You will not weigh restaurant risotto on a date night. Good.

  • Prioritise a protein main.
  • Assume oil and sauces are more generous than home cooking.
  • Log a best-estimate entry the same day (search similar meals; slightly overestimate rather than pretend it was steamed air).
  • Return to precision at home. One estimated meal inside a tracked week still teaches you more than a month of “I’ll be good later.”

Fast-food rule of thumb: use the chain’s published numbers when available. Local café rule of thumb: pick a similar generic entry and add a buffer for oil. Pride about precise restaurant macros is usually fake precision. Directionally honest beats delusionally exact.

9) Alcohol budgeting

Decide before the first drink:

  • How many drinks?
  • What are they (beer, wine, spirits + mixer)?
  • What food will you cut or keep to stay near the weekly average?

Log the drinks. If sleep and training quality fall apart every time you drink, that is data — not a moral lecture.

A simple budget method: keep protein hit, reduce carbs/fats earlier in the day by roughly the drink calories, and stop when the planned number is done. “I’ll see how I feel” is how weekly averages get ambushed.

10) When to simplify to calories + protein only

Full macro theatre helps some people. It makes others quit by Thursday.

Simplify to calories + protein when:

  • tracking all three macros is killing adherence
  • you’re competent enough that fats/carbs flex without chaos
  • life load spiked (new baby, overtime, travel) and you need the minimum viable dashboard

Hit calories. Hit protein. Let carbs and fats fill the rest from foods you tolerate. That pair alone outperforms “I eat pretty clean” memory every time.

Simplifying is not failing. Quitting tracking entirely because you tried to micromanage every gram of fat on a fourteen-hour shift — that is the failure mode this rule prevents.

Hunger management in STRIP, social meals, and common mistakes

STRIP hunger is real. Pretending you can white-knuckle misery for forty weeks is how people rebound into chaos.

Hunger management that actually helps

  • High protein at every feed.
  • High-volume veg and fruit for chew and fullness.
  • Potatoes and other satisfying carbs beat living on sad salad energy.
  • Diet soft drinks / coffee / tea (within your caffeine-sleep trade-offs) as tools, not personality.
  • Steps — walk more; it helps expenditure and often mood.
  • Sleep — short sleep makes hunger louder and adherence dumber.
  • Keep tasty foods in the plan in measured amounts so you are not building a forbidden-food explosion for Saturday.
  • Protein desserts (yoghurt + cocoa, high-protein pudding hacks) when sweet cravings show up.

Hunger has flavours. Learn which one you’re dealing with:

Feeling Often means Better move
Empty + shaky + obsessed Deficit too deep, carbs too low, or long gap between feeds Bring a feed forward; raise calories if persistent
Bored mouth / “want something” Habit, stress, salt/crunch craving High-volume crunchy veg, gum, tea, planned snack
Night raiding Under-ate earlier, poor dinner protein, sleep debt Bigger daytime protein + solid dinner; protect bedtime
Post-alcohol hunger Disinhibition + poor sleep Plan drinks; protein-first next morning; no spiral

If hunger is unbearable and training is falling apart, your deficit is too aggressive or your food selection is too low-volume. Fix the inputs. Suffering is not proof of progress.

Social meals without blowing the week

  • Eat protein-forward; enjoy the occasion; estimate and log.
  • Front-load the week with tighter adherence if you know a big dinner is coming — or just accept one higher day inside a solid weekly average.
  • Do not “earn” social meals with punishment cardio cosplay that ruins recovery.
  • Do not turn one dinner into a four-day off-plan spiral. The spiral is the problem, not the steak.

Family BBQs, work lunches, and kids’ leftovers are part of adult life. The skill is staying in the weekly average without becoming the person who brings a tub of weighed chicken to every event — unless that is genuinely your preference and your social life can handle it. Stage 5C — STRIPLINE Programmes: PPL & PPLU

This is the training chapter you run, not the one you skim for vibes.

Stage 5 already told you the rules: progressive overload, hard-enough sets, log the work, protect recovery. This chapter installs the default STRIPLINE programmes — the templates you put on the calendar tomorrow morning.

Default recommendation (non-negotiable clarity):

Your reality Default programme Weekly shape
You can train 6 days and recover (sleep mostly OK, stress manageable) PPL — Push / Pull / Legs PPL → rest or light day → PPL → rest
You can train 4 days hard, or 6 days feels like fantasy PPLU — Push / Pull / Legs / Upper Four training days; three rest or step days
Life will not allow 4+ days this season 3-day full body (fallback) Mon / Wed / Fri or any three non-adjacent days

Not “3–4 days vaguely.” Pick one. Lock it for 8–12 weeks. Stop inventing a new split every Sunday night.

PPL and PPLU are the primary STRIPLINE programmes because they hit each major muscle roughly twice per week, keep sessions themed and short enough to finish, and scale from beginner to intermediate without rewriting the skeleton. The 3-day full-body plan is the honest backup when schedule or recovery says no.

What “default” means here

These templates are evidence-aware defaults: each major muscle hit about twice weekly when you run PPL or the Push+Upper pairing in PPLU; compounds first; accessories that actually progress; effort near failure without living in form collapse; progressive overload you can measure. They are not a secret private coach sheet photocopied into a book. Where an exact coached prescription would matter and we don’t have clearance to invent one, you’ll see a sparse placeholder — not a fake protocol dressed up as gospel.

Year one does not require novelty. It requires attendance, hard sets, and a logbook that proves next week was a little better than last week.

How to choose: PPL vs PPLU vs 3-day

Choose PPL when

  • You can guard six training days most weeks (construction, shift work, kids — if you’ve got the slots, use them).
  • Sessions can be 45–70 minutes.
  • Sleep is mostly decent and you’re not already living in a deep deficit crash.
  • You like theme days: push day feels like push day.

Typical PPL week:

Day Session
Mon Push
Tue Pull
Wed Legs
Thu Rest or long walk
Fri Push
Sat Pull
Sun Legs

That’s two full PPL cycles. Each muscle gets hit twice. Rest Thursday (or move the rest wherever life needs it). Don’t skip Legs twice and call it “prioritising upper.”

Choose PPLU when

  • Four hard days is what you’ll actually hit.
  • Six days sounds heroic and you already know you’ll ghost Fridays.
  • You’re in a hard STRIP deficit and six hard sessions leave you flat.
  • You’re newer and want slightly more recovery between hard lower-body days while still covering upper twice.

Push + Upper covers chest/shoulders/triceps twice. Pull covers back/bis. Legs once hard — and Upper still includes some rear-delt / upper-back work so the week doesn’t feel half-empty. If you want legs twice on a four-day plan later, you can swap Upper for a second lower day — but PPLU as written is the default four-day STRIPLINE template.

Choose 3-day full body when

  • You cannot protect four days.
  • You’re restarting after a long layoff and need practice without burying yourself.
  • Travel, overtime, or childcare makes PPL/PPLU a lie this month.

Three hard full-body days beat six skipped half-sessions. Run it without shame. Upgrade when the calendar allows.

Recovery check (be honest)

If joints ache, sleep is wrecked, and every session is junk for two weeks straight — you don’t need a fancier split. You need fewer hard sets, more food or sleep, or a MED week (below). Programme choice sits under recovery, not above it.

Real-life schedule examples (steal one)

Early shift / construction-style days: train after work three nights + weekend mornings for PPLU; or early AM short sessions for PPL if you’ve got the discipline and sleep isn’t toast. If 4 a.m. wake-ups already exist, don’t add hero PPL on five hours of sleep — pick PPLU.

Office / hybrid: Push Mon lunch, Pull Tue after work, Legs Thu morning, Upper Sat. Rest Wed/Fri/Sun. That’s a clean PPLU.

Kids / evenings only: three weeknights + one weekend day. If a weeknight dies, the weekend becomes the make-up — not a free pass to skip the pattern.

Shift work: map the split to your waking days, not the calendar’s Mon–Sun ego. Two Push / two Pull / two Legs across your work cycle for PPL; or four quality sessions across the roster for PPLU. Consistency of stimulus beats matching Instagram’s week.

Travel month: run 3-day full body with hotel DBs / bands, or MED Push/Pull/Legs in 30–40 minutes. Resume the locked template when you land — don’t “try a new split I saw.”

Switching between PPL and PPLU mid-block

Allowed when life actually changes — not when you’re bored.

  • Downgrade PPL → PPLU if you miss two+ sessions per week for two weeks, or recovery collapses.
  • Upgrade PPLU → PPL only after 4+ weeks of hitting all four days, sleep is fine, and you want more frequency — not because a mate trains six days.
  • Keep as many exercise selections as you can when you switch so the logbook still means something.

Session structure (every training day)

Same skeleton for PPL, PPLU, and full body.

1. Warm-up (8–12 minutes)

  • 3–5 minutes easy cardio or brisk walk into the gym.
  • Dynamic bits for the day’s joints (arm circles, hip openers, bodyweight squats — don’t invent a yoga class).
  • Ramp sets on the first compound: empty bar / light machine → add load → one last light–moderate set. Ramp sets are not work sets. Don’t count them as volume.

2. Work sets

  • Hit the listed sets × reps.
  • Most working sets: about 1–2 reps in reserve (RIR) — near failure, form still honest.
  • Last hard set of an isolation can flirt with 0–1 RIR if the joint feels fine.
  • Stop a set when form collapses into ego theatre. That rep didn’t count anyway.

RPE quick map (use one language in the logbook):

Feel RIR RPE (approx.)
Could do 3+ more clean reps 3+ ≤7
Could do ~2 more 2 ~8
Could do ~1 more 1 ~9
No clean reps left 0 10

Target most compounds at RPE 8–9 once you’ve learned the movement. Week 1 beginners: leave a little more in the tank while you find the groove — still not polite gym tourism.

3. Rest times

Lift type Rest
Heavy compounds (squat, hinge, press, row) 2–3 minutes (up to 3–4 if truly heavy)
Machines / secondary compounds 90–150 seconds
Isolations (curls, laterals, pushdowns) 60–90 seconds

Rest is part of the programme. Scrolling for four minutes between lateral raises is not “intensity.”

4. Logging (non-optional)

Every session, write:

  • Exercise
  • Load
  • Sets × reps actually done
  • Rough RIR / RPE on the hard sets
  • One-line note if something hurt or sleep was rubbish

Phone notes app, notebook, spreadsheet — pick one. If it’s not logged, you didn’t progress; you exercised.

5. Finish

You’re done when the list is done. No bonus burnout circuit unless the template says so. Save energy for the next session.

Finding week-1 loads (so you can train tomorrow)

You do not need a one-rep max calculator. You need a starting number that lets you learn the movement and still work hard by the end of the week.

  1. Guess light. First working set should feel like you could do the top of the rep range easily.
  2. Adjust set to set. If set 1 was 12 reps with four left in the tank on an 8–12 lift, bump the load for set 2. If set 1 died at 5 reps with ugly form, strip weight.
  3. Lock a load for next time. By the end of session one, you should know “this is my working weight for bench right now.” Write it down.
  4. Week 1 bias: leave ~2–3 RIR while grooves form. Weeks 2–3: push most sets to ~1–2 RIR.
  5. Machines vs free weights: machines often let you push closer to failure sooner because the path is fixed — still don’t bounce into joint pain.

If you are returning after months off, start more conservatively than your old PRs. Pride is not a warm-up set.

What to bring / how to use the gym floor

  • Water, headphones optional, logbook mandatory.
  • Claim a rack or bench for the main lift; do accessories that don’t steal someone else’s compound station for forty minutes of laterals.
  • If a machine is taken, do the next free exercise in the same pattern, then return — don’t stand still for ten minutes protecting a seat with a towel and a glare.
  • Train the planned day even if the gym is busy. Busy is not a programme change.

Effort and form cues (coach voice, not textbook)

Chest presses: shoulder blades set, wrists stacked, lower with control, drive without bouncing the bar off your ribs like a trampoline.

Overhead work: ribs down, don’t turn every press into a standing crunch. If your lower back is doing the lift, the load is too heavy or the setup is lazy.

Rows / pull-downs: pull elbows, not just hands. Pause a beat at the squeeze. Don’t cheat every rep into a hip-hinge Olympic event.

Squats / leg press: depth you own, knees tracking roughly over toes, no ego depth that turns into a lower-back story. Full range you control beats half-reps with a plate you can’t earn.

Hinges (RDL / back extension): soft knees, push hips back, feel hamstrings/glutes — not a rounded spine speed-run.

Lateral raises: slight elbow bend, lead with elbows, stop before shrugging the weight to your ears.

Curls / extensions: own the negative. Swinging the torso for the last three “reps” is cardio with a dumbbell.

Effort rule: if you finish every set knowing you had four clean reps left, you didn’t train — you visited. If every set ends in a screaming form dump, you didn’t train — you performed. Live in the middle: hard, clean, progressive.

Breathing (simple): brace before the hard part of the rep — don’t hold a purple face for a twelve-rep set. Exhale through the sticking point on presses and squats; stay braced enough that your spine doesn’t become a garden hose.

Tempo default: controlled down (roughly 2 seconds), crisp up. No need for fancy 4010 codes. If you bounce every rep, slow the eccentric until you own it.

Pain vs effort: muscle burn and the urge to quit near the end of a hard set is normal. Sharp joint stabbing, unusual nerve zings, or pain that alters your gait walking out of the gym is not “the programme working.” Deload that pattern, swap the variation, get it checked if it persists.

Film one set per week of your hardest compound. Watch it once. Fix one thing. That’s coaching without a coach.

Progressive overload: double progression

This is the engine. The split is just the calendar.

Rule (default):

  1. Pick a rep range (usually 8–12 for most hypertrophy work; 6–10 on some heavier compounds if you prefer).
  2. Use a load you can hit the bottom of the range for all prescribed sets at ~1–2 RIR.
  3. When you hit the top of the range for all work sets with solid form, add load next session (smallest plate jump that makes sense — often 2.5 kg total on dumbbells/machines, 2.5–5 kg on barbells).
  4. New load will often drop you back toward the bottom of the range. Climb again.

When you stall for 2–3 sessions on a lift

  1. Check sleep, protein, and whether you’re actually near failure.
  2. Keep the load; earn the reps.
  3. If still stuck: small form fix, longer rest, or swap to a close variation (e.g. DB press → machine press) and progress that.
  4. Do not add five random sets of junk.

What counts as overload (and what doesn’t)

Counts: more load at same reps; more reps at same load inside the range; same load/reps with cleaner deeper range you previously shortened; controlled pauses that make the set harder on purpose when plates don’t exist.

Doesn’t count: longer sessions with gossip between sets; random drop sets every week that aren’t logged; swapping the whole lift because last session felt hard; “I sweated more.”

Deload rules

Plan the deload so you don’t invent one after an injury.

Default: every 6–8 weeks of hard training, or earlier if sleep tanks, joints complain, and performance slides for ~7–10 days despite honest effort.

Deload week options (pick one):

  1. Same exercises, ~50–70% usual load, same or slightly fewer sets, stop at ~3–4 RIR.
  2. Keep load moderate, cut sets roughly in half.
  3. MED week (below) if life is chaos — still counts as training, not failure.

Deload is not “seven days of couch and guilt.” Walk. Sleep. Eat protein. Come back hungry to train — not wrecked.

Do not deload every time the scale is noisy. Deload for fatigue and performance, not for feelings after one bad session.

Steps, cardio, and the lifting week

STRIP loves steps. Lifting still owns the calendar.

  • Default: get steps on rest days and after sessions if needed — walking is not a substitute for Pull day.
  • Cardio: 1–2 easy Zone-2 style sessions (20–30 minutes) is plenty for most year-one lifters if steps are already solid. Put them on rest days or after upper sessions, not before heavy Legs when it nukes your squat.
  • Hard conditioning every day plus six-day PPL plus a deep deficit is how people stall, sleep badly, and blame genetics.
  • Hardgainers in LIFT: watch that cardio doesn’t quietly erase the surplus. Lift hard, walk some, eat.

Steps targets live in Stage 5’s playbook. Here the rule is simpler: never cancel a programmed Lift day to “do more cardio instead” unless a coach or clinician has a specific reason. Fat loss is mostly the food deficit plus consistency; the programme builds the muscle you’re trying to keep.

Running an 8–12 week block (don’t freestyle)

  1. Weeks 1–2: learn loads, grooves, calendar. Effort climbing toward 1–2 RIR.
  2. Weeks 3–6: double progression in full swing. Same exercises. Log everything.
  3. Week 7 (or 6–8): deload if fatigue is real — or keep rolling if you feel strong and performance is still climbing. Don’t deload from boredom.
  4. Weeks 8–12: resume hard training. Add at most one set to a lagging lift if recovery is clearly fine. Optional small exercise swap only if a joint hates a movement — same pattern, new tool.
  5. End of block: photos, waist, strength notes. Decide: stay on the same split another block, or switch PPL ↔ PPLU for life reasons — not vibes.

FAQ

Do I have to get stage-lean before I build any muscle?
No. You lift during STRIP. Fat-loss-first means fat is the priority bias — not “never stimulate muscle.”

I’m skinny. Shouldn’t I bulk?
Only if photos show you’re already lean. Soft middle + “I’m a hardgainer” is usually skinny-fat cosplay. Document exceptions; don’t invent them.

How aggressive should the deficit be?
Moderate enough to train and adhere. Start ~300–500 kcal below maintenance for many people; adjust from trend data.

Can I recomp instead of cut?
Often yes early — especially skinny-fat — if lifting and protein are excellent and the middle still improves. Recomp isn’t a forever fog to avoid decisions.

What if I miss a week?
Run MED. Resume. Don’t burn the programme and start a new identity.

How much protein?
~1.6–2.2 g/kg as a practical band. Use Food Bro + book hierarchy. Consistency beats precision theatre.

Is cardio required?
Steps help a lot in STRIP. Extra cardio is optional if it doesn’t trash lifting or recovery.

When do I move to LIFT emphasis?
When midsection trajectory and adherence criteria you wrote are met — not when boredom hits.

Do I need supplements?
Optional. Creatine, caffeine, protein powder are the sensible tier once basics exist.

What about PEDs / peptides / GLP-1s?
Read the appendix for literacy. No protocols in this book. Fundamentals first. Discussion of personal fit: [PLACEHOLDER: paid offer / coach funnel — pending agreement].

How do I track without obsessing?
Photos + waist + strength + weekly averages. Simplify if the app runs your mood.

What if my job destroys my schedule?
Shorter sessions, fewer days, protein defaults, step floor, LINK still active. Busy is normal here — chaos programmes aren’t.

GLP-1s in 2026 — overweight, doctors, and realistic expectations

What this class is (plain language)

GLP-1 pathway medicines (and related dual/triple agonists people hear about) affect appetite, satiety, gastric emptying, and metabolic signalling. For many people under medical care, average calorie intake drops without relying only on white-knuckle willpower. That can create a real fat-loss environment.

Legitimate pathway: doctor + overweight/obesity care

If someone is truly overweight or living with obesity, in many places a doctor can assess and may prescribe a GLP-1 (or related agent) as part of medical weight management — depending on criteria, comorbidities, local guidelines, supply, and cost/coverage. That is a clinical pathway, not a gym hack.

What “legitimate” looks like in practice (themes, not instructions):

  • Assessment of BMI/adiposity, history, contraindications, other meds
  • Prescription and follow-up through a qualified prescriber
  • Monitoring of tolerance, side effects, and progress
  • Lifestyle still exists — medication is not a personality transplant

What it is not: buying a mystery pen because a reel promised stage-ready abs by summer.

What’s realistic vs marketing

Realistic: appetite drops; adherence to a deficit gets easier for some; scale weight can move faster than with lifestyle alone; some people finally get a runway after years of failed white-knuckle cuts.

Marketing fog: “no effort,” “no muscle loss if you wish hard,” “anyone should be on one,” “this replaces training,” “stop when you like and keep the look with zero habits.”

Composition — not just the scale

Analyses of GLP-1-assisted weight loss often show some absolute lean-mass drop as total weight falls. That isn’t an automatic catastrophe label — it’s why protein + progressive lifting stay non-negotiable if you care about looking athletic rather than only lighter.

Practical literacy while appetite is suppressed (behaviour layer — not a prescribing guide):

  1. Schedule protein — don’t wait to “feel like eating.”
  2. Keep a written lifting plan (PPL/PPLU from Stage 5C). MED weeks beat zero weeks.
  3. Don’t chase heroic weekly drops if strength is falling off a cliff — talk to your prescriber/coach frame; adjust the boring half.
  4. Steps help; punishment cardio on empty is how you get flat and injured.
  5. Photos + waist + lifts — the scale will lie cheerfully.
  6. Plan the off-ramp behaviours — diet pattern, sleep, training calendar — before you need them. Medication without lifestyle still fails the long game.

Where GLP-1s sit inside STRIPLINE

STRIP is fat-loss-first. A medically supervised GLP-1 can be a tool that makes STRIP adherence easier for the right patient — it does not skip LINE (locked plan) or LINK (accountability). If you’re not in a medical category for these drugs, don’t invent a DIY pathway because bodybuilding Instagram normalised pens.

Investigational or newer agents you hear about in 2026 may have different approval/access status by country and month. Treat headline hype as headline hype. Your clinician owns what’s actually available and appropriate.

Low testosterone and TRT clinics — education, not a starter kit

Why this comes up so often

Low testosterone (clinically evaluated hypogonadism — not “I had a nap and felt unmotivated”) is a real medical issue for some men. Symptoms people describe in the wild — low energy, low libido, mood changes, training recovery feeling broken — overlap with sleep debt, depression, obesity, alcohol, and overtraining. That’s exactly why bloodwork and a proper assessment matter instead of self-diagnosis from a podcast.

Why TRT clinics exist

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) clinics and endocrine pathways exist because some people have documented low testosterone (and a clinical picture that fits) and benefit from supervised replacement. Clinics also exist because demand is high and marketing is loud — which means you must still separate:

This book is not telling you that you need TRT. It is telling you that clinically low T is a legitimate medical domain, clinics exist for that domain, and gym lore is a terrible laboratory.

What “low T” literacy includes (no protocols)

  • Symptoms are non-specific — fix sleep, alcohol, body fat, and training stress before you crown yourself hypogonadal.
  • Confirmation is lab + clinical, not vibes.
  • Obesity and metabolic health interact with the androgen axis — sometimes fat loss improves the picture; sometimes medicine is still discussed. Neither sentence is a DIY prescription.

  • Monitoring themes in supervised care often include how you feel, blood counts/haematocrit trends, hormones as directed by the clinician, blood pressure, and follow-ups — exact panels are the clinician’s call.
  • “TRT” in gym speech sometimes means replacement for low levels and sometimes means physique-dosed androgens with a clinic sticker. Literacy means asking which one you’re actually talking about.

TRT vs physique androgen use

Replacement for diagnosed deficiency and “I want larger delts” are not the same project ethically, medically, or in monitoring intensity. Blurring them is how people get hurt or scammed. If your goal is physique enhancement beyond replacement, you’re in the next section’s risk conversation — not a soft TRT rebrand.

Legacy craft notes — GLP-1 / metabolic (literacy extract)

Reframed under STRIPLINE literacy — no protocols. Prefer the main sections above if anything conflicts.

These drugs changed the game. Appetite drops. Adherence gets easier. Scales move. Used with a brain — training, protein, monitoring, ideally a coach — they can be transformational. Used as a “stop eating and disappear” button, you get smaller and softer and wonder why you hate the mirror.

Lived reality check: tools in this family (including retatrutide in the emerging lane, and testosterone elsewhere in Part Four) have been remarkably positive for people who finally got fat loss and hormones moving after years of white-knuckle failure. Upside is real. So is the skinny-soft trap if you drop the barbell.

WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW

What they do

GLP-1 pathway drugs (and dual/triple agonists around them) hit appetite, satiety, gastric emptying, metabolic signalling. You eat less without relying only on monk willpower. That creates a real deficit. Treat it like a powerful adherence tool, not a personality transplant.

Done properly vs scale cosplay

Properly Chaos
Goal = better composition, not just a number Fastest possible drop
Protein planned on purpose (hunger is gone — that’s the trap) “I’m not hungry so I skipped protein”
Progressive lifting non-negotiable Cardio-only vanishing act
Coach / clinician titration + follow-up Grey-market DIY + forum doses
Strength + photos tracked Scale as the only god

Muscle and lean mass — practical truth

Analyses of GLP-1/GIP agonist–assisted weight loss often show some absolute lean-mass drop (commonly ~¼ of total kg lost in pooled body-comp work) even when relative lean % looks steadier. That’s not an automatic disease label — it’s why you train. (Claim ; bib .)

Practical tips while appetite is suppressed:

  1. Protein first every day — schedule it; don’t wait to “feel like eating.” When intake collapses, lean hard into a high protein target (many coaches push toward that ~g/lb ballpark while cutting) so the deficit isn’t all lean tissue.
  2. Lift hard on a written plan — MED weeks beat zero weeks. Keep some progressive loading even on rough weeks.
  3. Don’t chase heroic weekly drops if strength is falling off a cliff.
  4. Steps help; punishment cardio on empty is how you get flat and injured.
  5. Watch session quality — if every workout is trash, food or recovery needs a nudge with your coach.
  6. Photos + waist + lifts — the scale will lie cheerfully.
  7. Structure > drug alone — sleep, food defaults, training calendar. Medication without lifestyle still fails the long game; if the only thing holding your look is appetite suppression 365 days a year with zero habits underneath, you’re renting the result.

Retatrutide deep dive (literacy + smart-use framing)

Next-wave multi-agonist interest is high because fat loss can be aggressive. Smart take:

  • Respect that access/approval status moves by country and year
  • Don’t copy another drug’s expectations onto a newer agent
  • Build the behaviour layer (protein, lifting, sleep) around what the drug actually does to appetite
  • Coach + medical oversight — not a group chat

Stopping without habits is how weight returns. That’s not a moral failing; it’s a systems failure. Fix the system — diet pattern, sleep hygiene, lifting — the same way you’d fix any other tool’s off-ramp. It’s never only the medication.

Side-effect reality (not a scare pamphlet)

GI issues are common conversation topics. Fatigue happens. Food aversions happen. Work with whoever is supervising you — don’t “push through” chest pain, severe persistent vomiting, or anything that feels medically wrong.

WHY IT MATTERS

Pathway C readers will see miracles and feel behind. Pathway A/B readers will misuse appetite suppression when they needed surplus or recomp. Smart use matches the tool to the goal and keeps the physique project intact.
